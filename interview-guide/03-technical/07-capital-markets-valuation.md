# Capital markets, capital raising & valuation

Covers DCF, residual land value (static and discounted), comparables, structured equity, and fund waterfalls. For roles at IPC capital markets desks (ANAROCK, C&W, JLL), boutiques (Avener), funds (Kotak Alts, Godrej Ventures) and valuation firms (Sthapatya, Kroll, EY). All rates are **assumptions**.

## Primer: concepts before mechanics

**Three ways to value real estate:**
1. **Sales comparison (comps).** What have similar assets sold for? Adjust for differences (location, size, road width, FSI, approvals, timing). Best for land and flats where transactions exist. Weakest when comps are few or not truly comparable.
2. **Income approach.** For income assets: direct capitalisation (NOI ÷ cap rate) or a **DCF** (discount future cash flows plus a terminal value). For development land: the **residual method** (value of what can be built, minus the cost of building it and the required profit).
3. **Cost approach / DRC.** Land value plus depreciated replacement cost of buildings. Used for special-purpose assets and insurance, and as a floor.

**Residual land value (RLV): two versions that give very different answers.**
- **Static (margin-based):** RLV = revenue − costs (excluding land) − target profit (as % of revenue). On REF-W at a 20% margin target: **₹8.7 cr** for 2 acres.
- **Discounted (IRR-based):** RLV = NPV at the hurdle rate of all project cash flows except land. On REF-W at a 20% IRR hurdle: **₹19.4 cr**. At 18%: ₹21.5 cr. At 25%: ₹14.8 cr.
- They differ because a 20% margin on a 3.75-year project is a much tougher bar than a 20% annual IRR, and because construction-linked collections fund most of the cost. **Always state which hurdle you're using.** Promoters often think in margin, funds in IRR. The gap between the two is where land negotiations get stuck.

**Capital stack for a developer (lowest to highest cost):** customer advances (via construction-linked collections), bank CF, NBFC/HFC CF, structured debt/NCDs from AIFs, mezzanine, structured equity (with promoter buyback), and pure equity (JV partner, fund equity, IPO).

**Capital raising process (IPC/boutique):**
- **Mandate:** the developer hires the advisor. Success fee plus, sometimes, a retainer.
- **Teaser and IM:** project, developer, numbers, the ask.
- **Investor list.**
- **NDA and data room.**
- **Management meetings.**
- **Term sheets** (indicative, then binding).
- **Diligence:** legal, technical, financial and tax.
- **Definitive documents:** debenture trust deed or shareholders' agreement, security documents.
- **Close.**

**Fund economics:**
- **Management fee:** around 1.5–2% of commitments or invested capital.
- **Hurdle/preferred return:** 10–12%.
- **GP catch-up.**
- **Carried interest:** 15–20% of profits.
- **Gross IRR vs net IRR to LPs.**

## 15 interview questions with model answers

### Q1 [cap] Walk me through a DCF for a leased office building.
Project the cash flows for a holding period, usually 5–10 years. Start with the rent roll, lease by lease: escalations, renewal probability, downtime and re-letting costs at expiry, and market rent for vacant space. Deduct non-recoverable opex and a capex reserve to get NOI each year. At the end of the holding period, compute a terminal value by capitalising the next year's NOI at an exit cap rate, usually a little above the entry cap rate for prudence. Discount the annual NOI and the terminal value at a discount rate reflecting risk, e.g. 10.5–12% unlevered for Indian Grade A offices (assumption). Sum for present value. Check against direct capitalisation and comps. Remember that the terminal value is often 60–70% of total value, so the exit cap rate is the most sensitive input.

### Q2 [cap] What's residual land value, and what's the most common mistake in computing it?
RLV is the price a developer can pay for land and still meet its return target: the value of the completed development minus all costs other than land, minus the required profit. The most common mistake is not stating which hurdle is used. A static 20% margin on revenue and a 20% IRR give very different answers. On REF-W, the margin method gives about ₹8.7 cr for 2 acres and the IRR method about ₹19.4 cr. The IRR version rewards construction-linked collections and fast sell-through. The margin version ignores timing. Other mistakes: using FSI area instead of carpet for revenue, forgetting stamp duty on the land itself, and ignoring the time to get approvals, which delays everything.

### Q3 [cap] How would you value a land parcel for a sale mandate?
Triangulate. First, comps: recent land deals in the micro-market, converted to ₹ per FSI sq ft, adjusted for road width, size, zone and approval status. IPC land reports and registered deeds give data points. Second, residual: what would a typical buyer (a listed developer, a mid-size local developer) pay given their own margin/IRR hurdles and cost structures? A listed developer with a brand premium can pay more. Third, strategic value: does the parcel fit a particular buyer's plans, like an adjacent site or an entry into the micro-market? The mandate price is usually set at the upper end of the RLV range for the most motivated buyer type, with the comps as a floor argument in negotiations.

### Q4 [cap] How do you adjust land comps?
Convert every comp to a common unit, usually ₹ per sq ft of permissible FSI area (including premium, TDR and ancillary where the road width allows), not ₹ per acre. Then adjust for: timing (market movement since the deal date); size (large parcels often trade at a discount per unit, and very small ones can too); access and frontage; approval status (approved land is worth more); title quality; and deal structure (an outright price isn't comparable to a JDA headline). Document each adjustment and its basis, and weight comps by similarity. A clean comp set of 3–5 good matches beats 15 poor ones.

### Q5 [cap] What's the difference between NCDs, mezzanine and structured equity in Indian real estate?
NCDs (non-convertible debentures) are the usual instrument for AIFs and credit funds lending to developers: listed or unlisted debt with a coupon (often part paid currently, part accrued), secured on project assets, receivables, and share pledges and guarantees. Mezzanine is subordinated capital with a higher return, often in NCD form but ranking below senior CF, sometimes with an equity kicker. Structured equity is equity in the project SPV with a promoter obligation to buy back at a pre-agreed IRR (for example 18–22%), plus upside sharing above that. It sits last in the stack but has contractual downside protection. The choice depends on the stage, regulations (banks can't fund land), existing lenders' consents, and the promoter's willingness to give up control.

### Q6 [cap] A developer says: "Why should I pay 18% when a bank lends at 10%?" Answer.
Because the bank won't fund what the developer needs right now. If the need is land, approvals-stage money, or refinancing of a stressed project, bank rules and risk appetite exclude it. At 18%, the developer is paying for the capital being available at the right stage, speed of execution, and flexibility on structure: bullet repayments, accrued coupons, no sales covenants in the early months. The real comparison isn't 18% vs 10%. It's 18% debt vs 25%+ equity from a JV partner, or vs not doing the deal. Once the project reaches CF eligibility (approvals in, construction starting), the expensive money should be refinanced with cheaper bank CF. A good structure plans that refinancing from day one.

### Q7 [cap] What's a preferred return and a catch-up in a fund waterfall?
In a typical fund waterfall, distributions go first to return LPs' contributed capital, then to pay LPs a preferred return (the hurdle), say 12% per year compounding. Then the GP catch-up: 100% of distributions go to the GP until the GP has received its carried interest share (say 20%) of all profits distributed so far. After that, remaining profits split 80/20 between LPs and GP. The preferred return protects LPs by ensuring the GP earns carry only if the fund beats the hurdle. The catch-up means that once the hurdle is cleared, the GP ends up with the full 20% of profits. Without a catch-up, the GP's share would be less than 20%.

### Q8 [cap] What's the difference between gross IRR and net IRR?
Gross IRR is the return on the fund's investments before fees and carry: the performance of the deals. Net IRR is what LPs actually receive after management fees, fund expenses and the GP's carried interest. The gap is typically 3–5 percentage points (assumption). For example, 20% gross might be 15–16% net. LPs care about net. Fund managers market both, and a sophisticated LP will ask how the gap is computed, including fee drag in the early years and the effect of the J-curve. In an interview, the point to make is that deal-level IRRs presented to an IC are gross and need a buffer above the fund's net target.

### Q9 [cap] What does an IM for a land sale include?
The executive summary (asset, location, the ask/process). Location analysis: connectivity, infrastructure pipeline, social infrastructure. Micro-market analysis: pricing, absorption, supply, comps. Land details: area, survey numbers, title summary (from the seller's advocate), zoning and DP remarks, road widths, reservations. Development potential: an indicative area statement under UDCPR/DCPR and the potential product mix. An indicative feasibility or residual value range (sometimes withheld from the IM). The process: timeline, bid format, contacts. Disclaimers. The IM's credibility rests on the area statement and title summary: buyers will check both, and errors kill trust fast.

### Q10 [cap] How do you value a developer (listed) at a high level?
Commonly with a sum-of-the-parts NAV: the PV of the residential pipeline (ongoing, planned, land bank) at a project level, the value of rental assets (NOI ÷ cap rate), the value of any services or other businesses, minus net debt. Adjust for execution risk and the timing of land-bank monetisation. The market also uses multiples: P/E, EV/EBITDA, P/B, and increasingly "pre-sales" momentum and business-development additions (GDV added). For a strategy role like Raymond Realty's, understand that GDV pipeline figures drive sentiment but are only as valuable as their conversion to cash. The NAV approach makes that explicit.

### Q11 [cap] Why do Indian residential funds prefer structured debt over equity?
The historical experience of 2010–2018: many equity investments in Indian residential projects suffered delays, cost overruns and weak exits, and equity returns disappointed. Structured debt offers contracted returns, security and priority, and still earns high single-digit to high-teen returns, depending on the risk. RERA has improved transparency, and developer consolidation has concentrated risk in stronger names, which also favours credit. Equity is still used for platform deals with strong developers, or for land. The mix swings with the cycle. When residential sales are strong, equity looks more attractive, since the upside is real.

### Q12 [cap] What is a platform deal?
A partnership between an investor and a developer to invest in multiple projects over time, with pre-agreed capital commitment, investment criteria, governance and economics, instead of negotiating each deal separately. Common in Indian real estate between global investors (sovereign funds, pension funds) and developers. Benefits: speed of deployment for the investor, a committed capital source for the developer, and alignment through co-investment. The key terms are the investment criteria (city, segment, returns), the developer's co-investment share, the promote (the developer's performance fee above hurdles), decision rights, exclusivity and exit mechanisms. The Kotak Alts 14th fund's ADIA anchor is a fund-level relationship, a different structure from a project-level platform ([Realty n More](https://realtynmore.com/kotak-alts-secures-1-billion-adia-and-nps-korea/)).

### Q13 [cap] Explain the terminal value's weight in a DCF and how to handle it.
In a 5-year DCF of a stabilised office, the terminal value is often around 70% of the present value. In my example (NOI ₹32.8 cr growing 5%, 8% exit cap, 11% discount rate), TV is 70% of the ₹443 cr PV. So the exit cap rate and the final-year NOI assumption dominate the valuation. To handle it: use an exit cap rate at or above the entry cap rate unless there's a clear reason for compression; normalise the terminal-year NOI (no one-off spikes, and include a capex reserve); run sensitivities on exit cap and growth; and cross-check the implied value per sq ft against comps. If the DCF only works with cap-rate compression at exit, flag it.

### Q14 [cap] How would you check whether an 18% target IRR on a structured equity deal is realistic?
Look at the source of repayment. The promoter's buyback obligation is only as good as the project's cash flow and the promoter's balance sheet. So model the project: does it generate enough cash by the buyback date to pay 50 × 1.18³ ≈ ₹82 cr on a ₹50 cr investment over 3 years? Check the timing of approvals and launch, and the sales assumptions. Then check the promoter's group leverage and other obligations. Is this investment senior or junior to other claims? Also check the security: share pledge, mortgage, escrow. If the project cash flow comfortably covers the buyback in the downside case, 18% is realistic. If it depends on price escalation or fast sales, it isn't.

### Q15 [cap] Why might a landowner prefer to sell through an IPC-run process rather than directly?
Price discovery: a competitive process with multiple bidders usually beats a bilateral negotiation. Credibility: an IPC's IM and area statement reduce buyers' uncertainty, so bids are firmer. Reach: IPCs know which developers and funds are actively buying in the micro-market. The market data C&W and JLL publish, such as 3,093 acres of land deals in 2025 ([JLL](https://www.jll.com/en-in/newsroom/developers-secure-3093-acres-for-inr-92200-cr-investment)), shows how active the buyer pool is. Process management: data room, diligence coordination, negotiation of documents. The IPC's fee (1–2%: assumption) is typically recovered through better price and certainty. Direct sales still happen when the buyer is obvious, like an adjoining developer, or confidentiality is critical.

## 5 numeric problems

### P1 [cap] Discounted RLV on REF-W at different hurdles
**Given:** REF-W quarterly unlevered cash flows excluding land and stamp (₹17.12 cr at Q0), keeping ₹2 cr of early approvals at Q0. Discount at the hurdle IRR.

**Solution (NPV of cash flows excluding land, then ÷ 1.07 for stamp duty):**
- 14%: NPV ₹27.99 cr → max land **₹26.2 cr**
- 18%: ₹22.99 cr → **₹21.5 cr**
- 20%: ₹20.75 cr → **₹19.4 cr**
- 25%: ₹15.81 cr → **₹14.8 cr**
- Compare the static margin method: 20% margin → ₹8.7 cr. 16% margin → ₹16 cr.

**Point to make:** ₹16 cr land gives REF-W a 23.6% IRR but only a 16% margin. Whether the deal "works" depends on which hurdle the firm uses. Ask at the start of any case.

### P2 [cap] Land comps per FSI sq ft
**Given (assumptions):** three comps near Wagholi:
- A: 3 acres on a 24 m road (TDR 1.15), ₹30 cr.
- B: 1.5 acres on a 12 m road (TDR 0.65), ₹11 cr.
- C: 2 acres on an 18 m road (TDR 0.9), ₹17 cr.
Assume 5% deductions, basic 1.1 + premium 0.5, and 60% ancillary for all.

**Solution:**
- A: net 11,534 sq m × 2.75 × 1.6 = 50,749 sq m = 5,46,261 sq ft → ₹30 cr ÷ 5,46,261 = **₹549/FSI sq ft**
- B: net 5,767 sq m × 2.25 × 1.6 = 20,761 sq m = 2,23,471 sq ft → ₹11 cr ÷ 2,23,471 = **₹492/FSI sq ft**
- C: net 7,689 sq m × 2.5 × 1.6 = 30,757 sq m = 3,31,067 sq ft → ₹17 cr ÷ 3,31,067 = **₹513/FSI sq ft**
- Per acre, A (₹10 cr) looks 36% dearer than B (₹7.3 cr). Per FSI sq ft it's only 12% dearer.

**Point to make:** REF-W's ₹483/FSI sq ft is at the low end of this range, which supports the ₹8 cr/acre assumption (if the comps are real: they're illustrative here).

### P3 [cap] 5-year DCF of a leased office
**Given (assumptions):** NOI year 1 ₹32.79 cr, growing 5% a year. Exit cap 8% on year-6 NOI. Discount rate 11%.

**Solution:**
- NOI years 1–6: 32.79, 34.43, 36.15, 37.96, 39.86, 41.85
- TV at end of year 5 = 41.85 ÷ 0.08 = **₹523.1 cr**
- PV = Σ NOIₜ/1.11ᵗ (years 1–5) + 523.1/1.11⁵ = **₹443.0 cr**
- TV share of PV = **70%**

**Point to make:** compare with the direct cap value of ₹410 cr at 8%. The DCF is higher because 5% NOI growth is built in. Justify the growth: escalations alone give about 4.5–5% only if there's no downtime.

### P4 [cap] Structured equity buyback
**Given:** a fund invests ₹50 cr as structured equity. The promoter buys back after 3 years at a 22% IRR.

**Solution:** buyback = 50 × 1.22³ = 50 × 1.8158 = **₹90.8 cr**. Profit ₹40.8 cr, multiple **1.82×**.

**Point to make:** the question for diligence is whether the project generates ₹90.8 cr of free cash to the promoter by year 3 in the downside case. If not, the fund is relying on the promoter's other assets.

### P5 [cap] Fund waterfall
**Given (assumptions):** LPs contribute ₹100 cr. After exactly 3 years, total distributions are ₹180 cr. 12% compounding preferred return. 100% GP catch-up. 80/20 split thereafter.

**Solution:**
- Return of capital: ₹100 cr to LPs
- Preferred return: 100 × (1.12³ − 1) = **₹40.49 cr** to LPs
- Remaining: 180 − 100 − 40.49 = ₹39.51 cr
- Catch-up: GP gets 100% until GP = 20% of (pref + catch-up) → catch-up = 40.49 × 0.25 = **₹10.12 cr**
- Remaining ₹29.38 cr split 80/20 → LP ₹23.51 cr, GP ₹5.88 cr
- **GP total = 10.12 + 5.88 = ₹16.0 cr = 20% of ₹80 cr profit.** LPs get ₹164.0 cr → LP net multiple 1.64×, net IRR = 1.64^(1/3) − 1 ≈ **17.9%** (vs gross 180/100 → 21.6%).

**Point to make:** the gross-to-net gap here (21.6% → 17.9%) is from carry alone. Add management fees and the gap widens further.

## Common traps and wrong answers
- **Quoting RLV without stating the hurdle** (margin vs IRR).
- **Comparing land per acre** instead of per FSI sq ft.
- **A DCF with an exit cap rate below the entry rate** without justification.
- **Treating structured equity as "equity risk"** when it has a buyback, or as "debt" when the promoter's ability to pay is weak.
- **Forgetting the catch-up** when computing GP carry.
- **Presenting gross IRR as the investor's return.**
- **Treating a GDV pipeline as value.** It's gross revenue, before cost, timing and the landowner's share.
