# Land & title

Maharashtra context. Record names and forms are as generally used. Where a rule changes often, it's marked **verify current**. None of this is legal advice. In the interview, say "I'd confirm with the title advocate" when that's the honest answer.

## Primer: concepts before mechanics

**What "title" means.** Title is the legal right to own and transfer land. A developer buys land to build and sell flats, and buyers, lenders and RERA all rely on the developer having clear title. A title defect found after launch can freeze sales, trigger lender defaults and destroy the project's value. That's why land teams spend weeks on diligence before paying more than a token.

**Two record systems.**
- **Revenue records** (rural and peri-urban land, most of Pune's fringe). The **7/12 extract** (saat-baara) is two forms in one. Village Form 7 shows the holder, their rights, tenancy and encumbrances. Village Form 12 shows crops and cultivation. Every change of ownership or rights is recorded as a **mutation entry** (ferfar) in **Village Form 6**. The 7/12 cites the mutation numbers. The 7/12 is evidence of possession and revenue liability. It is not conclusive proof of title.
- **City survey records** (urban land). A **property card** is issued under a CTS (city survey) number.

**Title chain.** The sequence of transfers (sale deeds, inheritance, partition, gift, court decree) that leads to the current owner. Standard practice is to trace **30 years** of documents, but **verify the lender's or firm's policy**, as some want longer. The chain must match the mutation entries. Gaps (a sale deed without a mutation, or a mutation without a deed) are red flags.

**Search report.** An advocate searches the Sub-Registrar's index for registered transactions on the land (sales, mortgages, agreements) over the search period. Online search now covers recent years, but older years need physical searches (**verify current**).

**Title certificate.** The advocate's opinion that the title is clear and marketable, listing documents examined and conditions. Lenders and RERA registration rely on it.

**Land classes and restrictions that bite.**
- **Occupancy Class I vs Class II.** Class II land (e.g. granted, Inam, tribal-allotted, rehabilitated) needs Collector permission and often a nazrana payment before transfer.
- **Tenancy.** The Maharashtra Tenancy and Agricultural Lands Act: tenants may have purchase rights (Section 32G certificates). A sale by a tenant-purchaser may need permission under Section 43 (**verify current**).
- **Tribal land.** Transfers of tribal land to non-tribals are restricted under MLRC Section 36/36A (**verify current**).
- **Ceiling.** Old ULC (urban land ceiling) and agricultural ceiling orders can affect parcels.
- **Agricultural purchase restrictions.** Only agriculturists could historically buy agricultural land in Maharashtra. Conversion and exemptions matter (**verify current**).

**NA and zoning.** Agricultural land must be permitted for non-agricultural use. Maharashtra has simplified NA for land already in residential zones of a sanctioned development plan (deemed NA/conversion tax on zone basis: **verify current**). Zoning comes from the **Development Plan (DP)** or **Regional Plan (RP)**. The **zone certificate** or **DP remarks** tell you the land's zone, any **reservations** (school, garden, road) and **road widening** lines.

**Encumbrances.** Mortgages, liens, court attachments, lis pendens (pending suits), rights of way and easements. Check the 7/12 "other rights" column, the search report, and court databases.

**Due diligence, in order of what kills deals fastest:**
1. Zone/reservation/access (from DP remarks). If the land can't be built on, stop.
2. Ownership and all co-owners and heirs (the 7/12 plus the family tree). Missing heirs are the classic defect.
3. The 30-year chain plus search report.
4. Restrictions (Class II, tenancy, tribal, ceiling).
5. Litigation.
6. Physical: measurement survey (TILR), encroachments, boundaries.

**Commercial wrapper.** A land deal is usually an **MoU/term sheet**, then a **token** (1–10%), then diligence, then a **public notice** in newspapers inviting objections, then the **agreement for sale/conveyance** with payment milestones tied to conditions (NA, clearances, vacant possession), and finally **registration** with stamp duty.

## 15 interview questions with model answers

### Q1 [land] What is a 7/12 extract, and what does it not tell you?
The 7/12 extract is the Maharashtra revenue record for a survey number. Form 7 shows the holder(s), area, assessment, tenancy and other rights, including encumbrances like loans or government dues. Form 12 records cultivation and crops. It also references mutation entries that explain how the current entries arose. What it doesn't do is prove title. Entries are presumptive and can be rebutted. A registered sale deed that was never mutated won't appear. Neither will a pending civil suit, an unregistered agreement, or a family member's unrecorded share. So I read the 7/12 together with every mutation entry (Form 6), the registered deeds from the search report, and court searches, and confirm that the names, areas and dates reconcile across all three.

### Q2 [land] What is a mutation entry, and why do you read all of them rather than just the current 7/12?
A mutation entry (ferfar, Village Form 6) records a change in rights: a sale, inheritance, partition, loan charge, or a tenancy certification. The talathi records it and it's certified after notice to interested parties. I read the whole series because the current 7/12 shows the outcome, not the path. Defects hide in the path: an heir who wasn't included in a succession entry, a sale mutated without a registered deed, a certification made despite an objection, or an area that changes between entries without a partition order. Each mutation should tie to a document. If entry 1452 says "sale" but the search report shows no registered deed for that year, the chain has a hole that must be fixed before we pay more than a token.

### Q3 [land] Walk me through title diligence on a 2-acre parcel in Wagholi.
First, commercial feasibility: DP remarks for zone, reservations and road widening, and a site visit for access. If that passes, pay a small refundable token and start legal work. The advocate collects current 7/12s, all mutation entries, and 30+ years of deeds. They run a search at the Sub-Registrar and check court records. I'd map a family tree for every holder to catch heirs. Then check land class (Class I/II), tenancy history, and any ceiling or tribal restrictions. Commission a TILR measurement to match the physical area to records. Issue a public notice for objections. The output is a title report with a list of "curables": for example, releases from heirs, or a mutation to be corrected. The agreement ties payment milestones to curing each one.

### Q4 [land] What's the difference between a title search and a title certificate?
A search is the fact-finding step. The advocate inspects the Sub-Registrar's index for registered documents over the search period and records what's found: sales, mortgages, agreements, releases. A title certificate is the advocate's opinion, based on the search, the revenue records, and the documents in the chain. It says whether the title is clear and marketable, lists the documents examined, and states any conditions or exceptions. Lenders, RERA registration and buyers' banks rely on the certificate, but its value depends on the search behind it. So I'd read the certificate's exceptions and the list of documents examined. A clean certificate with a thin document list is weaker than a qualified one based on a full 30-year chain.

### Q5 [land] What is Class II occupancy land, and what does it mean for an acquisition?
Occupancy Class II land is held with restrictions on transfer. Typically it's land granted by government (for example Inam, Watan, rehabilitation or tribal allotments), or land that came under special tenures. Transfer usually needs the Collector's permission and payment of a nazrana (a premium, often a percentage of market value: **verify current**) to convert it to Class I. For an acquisition it means two things. There's a regulatory step and a cost that must be in the feasibility. And a sale without permission can be void, so the deal must be conditional on conversion. I'd make conversion a condition precedent before the main payment and budget both the premium and 6–12 months of time.

### Q6 [land] What is the significance of the Development Plan remarks?
DP remarks (or a zone certificate) come from the planning authority and state what the plan allows on the survey number. That covers the zone (residential, industrial, agricultural or green), reservations for public purposes (school, garden, hospital), proposed road widths and road-widening lines, and sometimes special notes like the blue/red flood lines near rivers. Commercially, they're the first filter. A reservation can take part of the land, though UDCPR often allows TDR or in-situ FSI in exchange (**verify current**). A proposed DP road can improve frontage and FSI eligibility but take land. A green or no-development zone can kill the deal. I'd get the DP remarks before spending on legal diligence.

### Q7 [land] What's a public notice and why is it issued?
Before completing a land purchase, the buyer's advocate publishes a notice in local newspapers (usually one English and one Marathi paper). It describes the land and the intended purchase and invites anyone with a claim to object within a set period, commonly 14 days. It flushes out unknown claimants: heirs, prior agreement holders, lenders, or tenants who don't show up in the records. It also creates evidence that the buyer acted in good faith. Objections received must be resolved before payment and registration, through release deeds, settlement or court process. It's cheap insurance. I'd never skip it, even on a parcel that looks clean.

### Q8 [land] How do you handle a missing heir discovered midway through diligence?
First, establish the facts. Build the family tree from death certificates, succession entries and legal heir certificates, and identify the heir's share. Then cure the defect in the least risky way. The best cure is a registered release deed, or the heir joining as a confirming party to the conveyance, with consideration paid directly to them. If the heir can't be traced or refuses, the options are court-based: succession proceedings, or a partition decree. Or the deal is restructured to exclude that share. Commercially, I'd hold back a retention from the sale price in escrow until the cure is complete, and extend the timeline in the agreement. Buying without curing puts the whole project's title at risk.

### Q9 [land] What is NA conversion, and how has it changed?
Agricultural land can't be used for construction without non-agricultural (NA) permission under the Maharashtra Land Revenue Code. Historically this was a separate, slow Collector process. Maharashtra has since simplified it. For land within residential or other non-agricultural zones in a sanctioned DP or Regional Plan, conversion is largely deemed on payment of conversion tax/charges, and the planning permission from the authority handles the rest (**verify current rules and charges**). For land outside such zones, a separate permission still applies. For a feasibility I'd confirm the zone first, budget the conversion charges, and not treat NA as the bottleneck it once was, while checking whether the parcel falls inside the deemed regime.

### Q10 [land] What's the risk in land with a Tenancy Act history?
Under the Maharashtra Tenancy and Agricultural Lands Act, tenants can become owners (deemed purchase on "tillers' day", certified under Section 32M). Land bought this way may have transfer restrictions: sale by such a purchaser may need Collector permission and payment under Section 43 (**verify current**). Risks: an old tenant claim that was never extinguished, or a transfer made without the required permission, which can be declared invalid. In diligence, I'd look for tenancy entries in the 7/12 "other rights" column and mutation entries mentioning 32G/32M. Then confirm that permissions were obtained for each transfer since. If not, the cure is permission plus payment before our purchase, as a condition precedent.

### Q11 [land] How do you structure payments to protect the developer?
Tie money to risk retirement. Typically: a token (1–5%) on MoU, refundable if diligence fails. A second tranche on a clean title report and public notice with no objections. The bulk on registration of the conveyance or development agreement, once conditions precedent are met: NA/conversion, Class II permission, releases, vacant possession. And a final retention (5–10%) held for defined post-closing items like mutation in the buyer's name or boundary settlement. Where litigation exists, part of the price sits in escrow until the suit is withdrawn or decided. Every tranche should be linked to a specific document, not to dates alone. The landowner will push back on retentions, and the negotiation is usually about the size and duration of each.

### Q12 [land] A broker offers land "with title clear, all papers ready". What do you ask for first?
Four things, before any meeting. The survey numbers and village, so I can pull 7/12s and mutation entries myself online. DP remarks or a zone certificate. A copy of the last registered deed. And the names of all current holders. With those I can do a desk check in an hour: are there multiple holders, any loan or tenancy entries, any reservation or road widening, and does the area match? I'd also ask who holds the mandate: is the broker authorised by all owners? Many "clear" parcels fail at step one because one co-owner hasn't agreed. Brokers aren't dishonest by default, but "all papers ready" isn't diligence.

### Q13 [land] What's title insurance, and is it used in India?
Title insurance pays for losses if a title defect emerges that wasn't known at purchase. It's standard in the US. In India it's available from some general insurers, and RERA discussed making it mandatory, but uptake has been limited (**verify current market**). In practice, Indian developers rely on diligence, indemnities from sellers, escrow retentions and representations in the agreement. I'd see title insurance as a supplement for specific residual risks, for example an old untraceable claimant. It's not a substitute for diligence. Insurers also exclude known defects, so it can't cure a problem you already know about.

### Q14 [land] How would you price land that has a pending civil suit?
Separate merits from economics. The advocate assesses the suit: what's claimed, the stage, the realistic outcome and timeline. I'd then price three things. First, time: how long until the suit is disposed of or withdrawn, and the carry cost on the land and on delayed launch. Second, probability of loss times its impact. Third, the cost of settlement, which is often cheapest. Structurally, pay a lower price upfront and hold a large retention in escrow released on withdrawal or a favourable decree. Or make the whole purchase conditional. Sunteck's Andheri East purchase shows it can be done: a site with over a decade of multi-party litigation was acquired once ownership was cleared ([Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/companies/news/sunteck-realty-buys-two-acre-andheri-east-land-with-2-500-crore-gdv-126012001134_1.html)).

### Q15 [land] What's the land cost per FSI square foot, and why do developers quote it?
It's land price divided by the buildable (FSI) area the land supports. Comparing land by ₹ per acre is misleading because two acres on a 9 m road and two acres on a 24 m road support very different FSI once TDR and premium are loaded. Per FSI sq ft, you're comparing what you pay per unit of sellable product. In REF-W, ₹16 cr for 3.32 lakh sq ft of FSI area is about ₹483/sq ft of FSI, or about ₹619 per sq ft of carpet. Developers then compare this with the sale rate: in mid-segment Pune, land at 8–15% of the carpet sale rate is often considered workable (**assumption**; REF-W is 8.3%).

## 5 numeric problems

### P1 [land] Stamp duty on an outright purchase in PMC limits
**Given (assumptions):** land price ₹16 cr. Pune (PMC) stamp duty 5% + 1% local body surcharge + 1% metro cess = 7% (**verify current**). Registration fee 1% capped at ₹30,000 (**verify current**). Market value per ASR is below the agreement value, so duty is on the agreement value.

**Solution:**
- Stamp duty = 7% × ₹16 cr = **₹1.12 cr**
- Registration = ₹30,000 (cap)
- Total ≈ **₹1.123 cr**, about 7% on top of the land price.

**Point to make:** if the ASR value were higher than the agreement value, duty would be on the ASR value. Always compute both. In a JDA, the development agreement itself attracts stamp duty (**verify current rate**).

### P2 [land] Land cost per acre → per FSI sq ft → share of sale rate
**Given:** REF-W. Land ₹8 cr/acre × 2 acres = ₹16 cr. FSI area 3,31,528 sq ft. Carpet 2,58,592 sq ft. Sale rate ₹7,500/sq ft carpet.

**Solution:**
- Per FSI sq ft = 16,00,00,000 ÷ 3,31,528 = **₹483**
- Per carpet sq ft = 16,00,00,000 ÷ 2,58,592 = **₹619**
- Land as % of sale rate = 619 ÷ 7,500 = **8.3%**

**Point to make:** if the same land were on a 12 m road (TDR cap lower, say 0.7 instead of 0.9: **verify table**), the FSI area would fall and the per-FSI cost would rise, with the same price per acre. Price per acre alone hides this.

### P3 [land] The holdout in the middle
**Given (assumptions):** you're aggregating 10 acres. 9 acres are signed at ₹6 cr/acre (₹54 cr). The last 1 acre sits in the middle and its owner asks ₹15 cr. Without it, the layout loses that acre plus 0.8 acre of efficiency (access, setbacks), leaving 8.2 usable acres. The residual land value (RLV, what the land is worth to you given the project) is ₹9 cr per usable acre.

**Solution:**
- With the holdout: cost = 54 + 15 = ₹69 cr for 10 usable acres → value 10 × 9 = ₹90 cr → surplus **₹21 cr**.
- Without it: cost = ₹54 cr for 8.2 usable acres → value 8.2 × 9 = ₹73.8 cr → surplus **₹19.8 cr**.
- Paying the holdout ₹15 cr (2.5× the others) still adds ₹1.2 cr of value. The break-even holdout price is 15 + 1.2 = **₹16.2 cr**.

**Point to make:** the correct comparison is total surplus, not the holdout's price per acre. Also, paying the holdout 2.5× may reopen negotiations with the other nine, if their agreements have most-favoured-nation clauses or aren't yet registered. Sequence the closings.

### P4 [land] Pricing litigation risk
**Given (assumptions):** land price ₹20 cr. The project's NPV before land is ₹40 cr, so deal NPV is ₹20 cr. There's a 10% chance a pending suit succeeds. If it does, the land is lost entirely, along with ₹5 cr already sunk in approvals and design.

**Solution:**
- Expected NPV (no protection) = 0.9 × 20 + 0.1 × (−20 − 5) = 18 − 2.5 = **₹15.5 cr**
- With a 25% retention (₹5 cr) held in escrow until the suit is disposed of and refunded if lost: loss case = −15 − 5 = −20 → EV = 18 − 2.0 = **₹16.0 cr**
- The escrow is worth ₹0.5 cr of expected value. It's also a strong incentive for the seller to settle the suit.

**Point to make:** the real value of an escrow is that it pushes the seller to cure the defect.

### P5 [land] Payment schedule vs upfront discount
**Given (assumptions):** the seller offers ₹16 cr on schedule: 10% now, 40% at 6 months, 50% at 12 months on conversion. Or ₹15 cr all upfront. Your cost of capital is 14% per year.

**Solution:**
- PV of schedule = 1.6 + 6.4/(1.14)^0.5 + 8.0/(1.14)^1 = 1.6 + 5.99 + 7.02 = **₹14.61 cr**
- Upfront = ₹15.00 cr
- The schedule is cheaper in PV by about **₹0.39 cr**, and it keeps money back until conversion, which also reduces risk.

**Point to make:** the upfront discount is only attractive if it's larger than about 8.7% (i.e. an upfront price below ₹14.61 cr) and title risk is fully retired.

## Common traps and wrong answers
- **"The 7/12 proves ownership."** It doesn't. It's presumptive revenue evidence. Always say "together with the chain and search".
- **Ignoring co-owners and heirs.** The most common real defect. Always ask "who else has a share?"
- **Comparing land per acre across different road widths or zones.** Convert to per FSI sq ft.
- **Paying a large token before DP remarks.** Zone, reservation and access come first.
- **Assuming NA is still a 12-month bottleneck everywhere.** Deemed conversion exists in DP residential zones (**verify current**). Equally, don't assume it applies everywhere.
- **Stamp duty only on agreement value.** It's on the higher of agreement value and ASR market value.
- **Forgetting the public notice.**
- **Treating title insurance as a cure.** It excludes known defects.
- **Bluffing regulation numbers.** Say "I'd confirm the current rate". Interviewers respect that more than a wrong number.
