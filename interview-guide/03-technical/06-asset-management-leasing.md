# Asset management & institutional leasing

Covers NOI, cap rates, yield on cost, WALE, lease structures, LRD and REITs. Rent and cap-rate figures are **assumptions** for Pune/Mumbai Grade A offices and warehousing in 2026. Refresh them from IPC quarterly reports (JLL, C&W, CBRE, Colliers, Knight Frank, ANAROCK) before interviews.

## Primer: concepts before mechanics

**Two ways to make money in real estate.** Build and sell (residential, mostly): value is realised once, at sale. Build and hold (offices, malls, warehouses, data centres): value comes from rent every year, and the asset's price is a multiple of that rent. Asset management is the discipline of maximising a held asset's income and value: leasing, renewals, cost control, capex, tenant mix, and eventually exit (sale, REIT or refinance).

**The income chain:**
- **Gross potential rent** = leasable area × rent rate × 12.
- **Less vacancy and credit loss** → effective gross income.
- **Plus recoveries** (CAM charges, parking), **less non-recoverable opex** (property tax the owner bears, insurance, management, leakage) → **NOI (net operating income)**.
- NOI excludes debt service, depreciation, income tax and (usually) capex.

**Valuation:** **Value = NOI ÷ cap rate.** The cap rate is the yield a buyer accepts. Lower cap rates mean higher value, and reflect lower risk and higher growth expectations. Indicative Indian Grade A office cap rates are roughly 7.5–8.5%, and warehousing roughly 7.5–8.5% (**assumptions**: check the latest IPC data and REIT valuation reports). A 0.5-point cap-rate move on ₹33 cr of NOI changes value by about ₹25–30 cr.

**Development yield:** **Yield on cost (YoC)** = stabilised NOI ÷ total development cost. If YoC exceeds the market cap rate, development creates value: build at 10.5% and sell at 8%.

**Lease terms (India, typical):**
- **Lease term** (e.g. 9 years = 3+3+3), with a **lock-in** (e.g. 3–5 years) during which neither side can exit, or the tenant pays the lock-in rent anyway.
- **Escalation:** 15% every 3 years, or about 4–5% annually.
- **Security deposit:** 6–10 months' rent for offices.
- **Fit-outs:** usually by the tenant, sometimes with landlord contribution or rent-free fit-out periods (2–4 months).
- **WALE** (weighted average lease expiry): average remaining lease term weighted by rent or area. Higher WALE means more stable income.

**LRD:** a loan against contracted rent (see `05-construction-finance.md`).

**REITs.** India has listed office and retail REITs (for example Embassy, Mindspace, Brookfield, Nexus: **verify current list**) regulated by SEBI. They must distribute at least 90% of net distributable cash flows. Most assets must be completed and rent-generating. Leverage is capped (**verify current limit**). For developers, REITs are an exit route: build, lease, stabilise, then sell into a REIT or sponsor one. **SM REITs** (small and medium REITs) were introduced for smaller assets (**verify current rules and size thresholds**).

**Institutional leasing** (one of the 12 functions): leasing offices, warehouses and retail to corporates. It's a sales function in form, but it's valued because it shapes asset value. Under CLAUDE.md screening rules, pure leasing brokerage is lower priority than asset management and underwriting.

## 15 interview questions with model answers

### Q1 [am] What's NOI, and what's excluded from it?
NOI is the property's income after operating costs: effective rental income (after vacancy and credit loss) plus recoveries like CAM and parking income, minus the operating expenses the owner bears (non-recoverable maintenance, property tax if borne by the owner, insurance, management fees). It excludes debt service, because financing is a choice of the owner, not a property characteristic. It excludes depreciation (non-cash) and income tax (owner-specific). Capex is usually excluded, but prudent underwriting deducts a capex reserve, since roofs, lifts and HVAC need replacing. The point of NOI is to measure the property's earning power independently of who owns it or how it's financed. That's why value = NOI ÷ cap rate works as a universal comparison.

### Q2 [am] What's a cap rate and what moves it?
The cap rate is NOI ÷ value: the unlevered first-year yield buyers accept for an asset. It's the market's price for risk and growth. It moves with interest rates (higher government bond yields push cap rates up), with the asset's risk (tenant quality, WALE, location, building grade, vacancy in the micro-market), and with growth expectations (strong rent growth justifies a lower cap rate). In India, Grade A offices in core business districts trade at lower cap rates than peripheral or older stock. Warehousing cap rates have compressed as institutional capital entered. The practical point: value is very sensitive to cap rates. Going from 8% to 7.5% on the same NOI raises value by 6.7%, which is why asset managers care about both income and market cap rates.

### Q3 [am] What's yield on cost and how do you use it?
Yield on cost = stabilised NOI ÷ total development cost, including land, construction, soft costs and finance. It's the developer's return on building an income asset, compared with the market cap rate at which the asset could be sold. If a warehouse costs ₹59.6 cr and earns ₹5.93 cr NOI when leased, YoC is about 10%. If the market cap rate for such assets is 8%, the completed asset is worth about ₹74 cr, a development profit of about ₹14.5 cr. The spread (YoC − cap rate) is the value creation. Developers typically need 150–250 basis points of spread to justify development risk (assumption). If the spread is thin, buy a completed asset instead of building.

### Q4 [am] What's WALE and why do investors care?
Weighted average lease expiry: the average remaining term of the leases in a building, weighted by rent (or area). Example: 40% of rent with 6 years left, 30% with 3, 20% with 1.5 and 10% with 8 gives a WALE of 4.4 years. Investors care because WALE tells them how long current income is contracted. A short WALE means re-leasing risk: vacancy, downtime, fit-out costs and possibly lower rents. A long WALE with strong tenants supports a lower cap rate and better financing terms, since LRD tenor usually follows lease tenure. I'd also look at the expiry profile. A WALE of 4.4 years can hide a cliff if 30% of rent expires in the same quarter.

### Q5 [am] What's the difference between lock-in and lease term?
The lease term is the total period of the lease, for example 9 years, often structured as 3+3+3 with renewal at the tenant's option. The lock-in is the initial period, for example the first 3 years, during which the tenant can't terminate. If they leave, they pay rent for the rest of the lock-in. After lock-in, the tenant can usually exit with notice (3–6 months). For valuation and lending, the lock-in is the only truly contracted income. Beyond it, income depends on the tenant choosing to stay. That's why lenders often size LRD on the lock-in period plus a renewal assumption, and why asset managers work on renewals well before lock-in expiry.

### Q6 [am] How would you value a leased office building quickly?
Take the rent roll: leasable area, rent per sq ft, occupancy. Compute gross rent, deduct vacancy and non-recoverable costs to get NOI, and divide by a market cap rate from recent transactions or REIT valuations. Example: 3 lakh sq ft at ₹110/sq ft/month, 90% occupied, 8% leakage: NOI ≈ ₹32.8 cr. At an 8% cap rate that's ≈ ₹410 cr. Then adjust: if leases are below market (reversion potential), value is higher. If a large lease expires soon, value is lower. A full valuation would be a 10-year DCF with lease-by-lease cash flows and a terminal value. The cap-rate method is the sanity check.

### Q7 [am] What's mark-to-market or rent reversion?
The gap between the rent a tenant currently pays and today's market rent for similar space. If in-place rent is ₹90/sq ft/month and market rent is ₹110, the lease is 18% under-rented. When it expires or is renewed, rent can rise to market: that's positive reversion. It's a key value driver for REITs and asset managers. On 1 lakh sq ft, a ₹20/sq ft/month uplift is ₹2.4 cr a year of rent. After 8% leakage and at an 8% cap rate, that adds about ₹27.6 cr of value. Reversion can also be negative: if leases were signed at the peak, renewal may mean lower rent or incentives to retain the tenant.

### Q8 [am] What's the difference between a built-to-suit and a speculative development?
Built-to-suit (BTS): a tenant commits to lease a building designed to its requirements before or during construction, usually with a long lease and lock-in. The developer has lower leasing risk and can raise construction and LRD financing more easily. The trade-offs are lower rent flexibility and a building that may be too specific to re-lease easily. Speculative development is built without a committed tenant, betting on market demand. Higher risk, higher potential rent and value if the market is strong. In warehousing on the Chakan corridor, both exist: BTS for large manufacturers, speculative for 3PL and e-commerce demand. The choice depends on the micro-market's vacancy and the developer's risk appetite.

### Q9 [am] Explain an Indian REIT to a promoter who owns three leased office buildings.
A REIT is a listed trust that owns rent-generating real estate and distributes most of its cash flow to unitholders (at least 90% of net distributable cash flows under SEBI rules). As a sponsor, the promoter would transfer stabilised leased buildings into the REIT in exchange for units and cash. That unlocks capital, gives liquidity and valuation transparency, and can realise a higher value because public investors accept lower yields than private buyers. Requirements include minimum asset size and a public float, a manager, a trustee, independent valuation, governance standards and leverage limits (**verify current SEBI REIT rules**). For three buildings, the size may fit an SM REIT better than a full REIT (**verify thresholds**). Downsides: loss of control, disclosure obligations and governance costs.

### Q10 [am] What does an asset manager do day to day?
Owns the business plan for each asset. That means leasing and renewals (tenant relationships, negotiating terms, managing expiries), budgets and NOI (opex control, recoveries, property tax), capex planning (lifts, HVAC, façade, ESG upgrades), and oversight of the property manager. They report monthly on occupancy, collections, arrears, NOI vs budget and WALE, and they're involved in financing (LRD renewals) and exit (sale or REIT). The job sits between operations and investment: improve income and value, and document it so the asset can be financed or sold at the best price. Asset managers are judged on NOI growth and value growth against the business plan.

### Q11 [am] How do you evaluate a tenant's credit for a long lease?
Financials (revenue, profitability, leverage, cash position) and credit ratings where available. Industry risk: IT/ITeS, BFSI or flex operators behave differently in downturns. Parent support (MNC parent guarantee vs Indian subsidiary alone). Occupancy history and how critical the location is to the tenant's operations: tenants rarely leave space where they've invested heavily in fit-outs. Security deposit size. For flex/co-working operators, look at their occupancy and the tenor of their own contracts, because they carry maturity mismatch risk. The goal is to judge how likely the tenant is to pay through the lock-in and renew. That drives the cap rate and LRD terms.

### Q12 [am] What's the typical structure of a Grade A office lease in Pune?
Typical (unverified per market, assumption): a 9-year term (3+3+3) or 5+5; lock-in 3–5 years; security deposit 6–10 months' rent; rent-free fit-out 2–4 months; escalation 15% every 3 years or about 4–5% annually; CAM charges billed separately at cost plus a margin; car parking charged per slot; the tenant bears fit-out capex, and the landlord delivers warm shell. Rent levels in Pune's core IT micro-markets (Kharadi, Hinjewadi, Baner, Yerwada) vary widely by building grade. I'd check the latest IPC Pune office report before quoting a rate. For warehousing, lease terms are often longer for BTS deals, with rent per sq ft much lower than offices.

### Q13 [am] What's the impact of a 50 bps cap-rate expansion on an asset worth ₹410 cr at 8%?
NOI is ₹32.8 cr (₹410 cr × 8%). At an 8.5% cap rate, value = 32.8 ÷ 0.085 ≈ ₹386 cr, a fall of about ₹24 cr, or 5.9%. If the asset carries an LRD of, say, ₹200 cr, equity value falls from ₹210 cr to ₹186 cr, an 11.4% hit to equity. That's the leverage effect. It shows why rising interest rates hurt real-estate equity values even when rents are stable, and why asset managers focus on growing NOI to offset cap-rate expansion. The same maths works in reverse: compression from 8% to 7.5% adds ₹27 cr.

### Q14 [am] How would you decide whether to renew a tenant at a discount or let the space go?
Compare the two cash flow paths. Renewing at, say, 10% below market means immediate continuity: no downtime, no brokerage, no fit-out contribution, and no rent-free period for a new tenant. Letting it go means downtime (say 6–9 months of vacancy), brokerage (typically 1–2 months' rent), a rent-free fit-out period, possibly a landlord contribution, and the risk that the market is softer than expected. Discount both paths over the lease term. A discount of up to roughly the value of 9–12 months' rent can be worth accepting to avoid the vacancy path. Also consider the tenant's credit and its effect on WALE and financing, since an LRD may require minimum occupancy.

### Q15 [am] Why would a residential developer care about asset management?
Three reasons. First, diversification: annuity income smooths the cyclicality of residential sales. Oberoi's rental portfolio is an example of a developer valuing that stability ([Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/markets/news/new-project-launches-rental-portfolio-to-drive-gains-for-oberoi-realty-123040400881_1.html)). Second, capital recycling: completed and leased commercial assets can be refinanced through LRD or sold to REITs, releasing capital for new projects. Third, mixed-use developments: retail and offices within residential townships raise residential values. A developer entering commercial must build asset-management capability: leasing, tenant relations, facility management and reporting. That's a different skill from selling flats. For land teams, the relevance is highest-and-best-use analysis: is this parcel worth more as residential for sale or as a leased asset?

## 5 numeric problems

### P1 [am] Value a leased office
**Given (assumptions):** 3,00,000 sq ft leasable in Kharadi. Rent ₹110/sq ft/month. Occupancy 90%. Non-recoverable opex and leakage 8% of effective rent. Cap rate 8%.

**Solution:**
- Gross potential rent = 3,00,000 × 110 × 12 = **₹39.6 cr**
- Effective (90%) = ₹35.64 cr
- NOI = 35.64 × 0.92 = **₹32.79 cr**
- Value at 8% = **₹410 cr**. At 7.5% = ₹437 cr. At 8.5% = ₹386 cr.

**Point to make:** the ±50 bps range covers ±₹25 cr, a bigger swing than filling half the vacancy (5% of ₹39.6 cr × 0.92 ÷ 0.08 ≈ ₹22.8 cr).

### P2 [am] Warehouse yield on cost (Chakan)
**Given (assumptions):** 10 acres at ₹2 cr/acre = ₹20 cr land. 2,00,000 sq ft built at ₹1,800/sq ft = ₹36 cr. Soft costs and finance 10% of construction = ₹3.6 cr. Rent ₹26/sq ft/month on the full area, pre-leased. NOI = 95% of rent. Market cap rate 8%.

**Solution:**
- Total cost = 20 + 36 + 3.6 = **₹59.6 cr**
- Rent = 2,00,000 × 26 × 12 = ₹6.24 cr. NOI = **₹5.93 cr**
- YoC = 5.93 ÷ 59.6 = **9.9%** (on gross rent, 10.5%)
- Value = 5.93 ÷ 0.08 = **₹74.1 cr** → development profit ≈ **₹14.5 cr (24% on cost)**

**Point to make:** the spread (9.9% YoC vs 8% cap) is about 190 bps, a healthy development spread. If land were ₹3 cr/acre, cost would rise to ₹69.6 cr and YoC to 8.5%. The spread nearly disappears. Land price decides warehousing feasibility.

### P3 [am] WALE
**Given:** four tenants by share of rent: 40% with 6 years left, 30% with 3, 20% with 1.5, 10% with 8.

**Solution:** WALE = 0.4×6 + 0.3×3 + 0.2×1.5 + 0.1×8 = 2.4 + 0.9 + 0.3 + 0.8 = **4.4 years**.

**Point to make:** 20% of rent expires in 18 months. Start renewal talks now, and check the LRD's minimum occupancy covenant.

### P4 [am] REIT distribution yield
**Given (assumptions):** REIT unit price ₹380. Annual distribution ₹24 per unit, of which about 60% is interest/dividend and the rest capital repayment (the tax character varies: **verify**).

**Solution:**
- Distribution yield = 24 ÷ 380 = **6.3%**
- Comparison: if the 10-year G-sec yields about 6.5–7% (assumption), the REIT yield is below bonds. Investors are paying for rent growth (escalations + reversion) and potential cap-rate compression.

**Point to make:** total return = distribution yield + growth. A 6.3% yield plus 4–5% NOI growth gives roughly 10–11% expected total return, before any valuation change.

### P5 [am] Value of rent reversion
**Given (assumptions):** 1,00,000 sq ft leased at ₹90/sq ft/month, expiring in 12 months. Market rent ₹110. Leakage 8%. Cap rate 8%. Discount rate for the one-year wait 10%.

**Solution:**
- Uplift in rent = 1,00,000 × 20 × 12 = ₹2.4 cr/year → NOI uplift = ₹2.21 cr
- Capitalised value uplift = 2.21 ÷ 0.08 = **₹27.6 cr** (realised on renewal)
- PV today = 27.6 ÷ 1.10 = **₹25.1 cr**, before allowing for downtime risk if the tenant leaves (e.g. 6 months of vacancy on ₹13.2 cr of new annual rent costs about ₹6.6 cr gross).

**Point to make:** reversion is real value, but it's contingent on the tenant renewing or on quick re-leasing. Model both paths.

## Common traps and wrong answers
- **Deducting interest or depreciation in NOI.**
- **Confusing cap rate with IRR.** The cap rate is a one-year yield. IRR includes growth and exit.
- **Treating the lock-in as the full lease term** in valuation, or vice versa.
- **Ignoring leakage, vacancy and non-recoverable costs** (valuing on gross rent).
- **Forgetting that a lower cap rate means a higher value.**
- **Assuming YoC above the cap rate automatically means "build".** Check the spread against development risk.
- **Quoting Indian REIT rules precisely without "verify current".**
