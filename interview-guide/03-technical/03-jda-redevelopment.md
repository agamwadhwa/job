# JDA & redevelopment structuring

Covers area share vs revenue share, outright vs JDA vs DM, Pune society redevelopment, and Mumbai 33(7)(B)/33(9)/33(10) basics. Regulation percentages are **verify current**. Tax and stamp treatment of JDAs is specialist ground: say "I'd confirm with tax" rather than guess.

## Primer: concepts before mechanics

**Why JDAs exist.** A landowner has land and wants its full value. A developer has the capability (approvals, construction, brand, sales) but doesn't want to tie up capital in land. In a Joint Development Agreement (JDA), the landowner contributes land and the developer builds. The two split the output. Both sides trade certainty for upside: the landowner accepts delay and execution risk for a larger share than an outright price, and the developer gives up margin for lower capital and a faster start.

**The three structures (developer's view):**

| | Outright purchase | JDA (area or revenue share) | Development management (DM) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who owns land | Developer | Landowner, with development rights to the developer | Landowner |
| Developer's capital | Highest (land + construction) | Construction + approvals only | Minimal |
| Developer's return | Full margin | Margin after landowner's share | Fee (e.g. 8–12% of revenue: assumption) |
| Who bears market risk | Developer | Shared | Landowner |

**Area share vs revenue share.**
- **Area share:** the landowner gets X% of the built carpet (specific flats) and sells them independently. Simple, and the landowner controls their own flats. Problems: two sellers in one project, with the landowner often undercutting on price; unit allocation fights (floor, view); and messier RERA and escrow.
- **Revenue share:** the landowner gets X% of sale proceeds (sometimes gross, sometimes net of GST and brokerage). The developer controls all inventory and pricing. It's cleaner for buyers, lenders and RERA. Listed developers prefer it. Lodha's Wakad JDA gives landowners **22.5% of project revenue** ([Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/companies/news/lodha-signs-development-agreement-for-20-468-sqm-plot-in-pune-s-wakad-125042400406_1.html)). In Raymond Realty's Parel JDA, about 40% of revenue goes to the landowner/partner, who also bears rehab and approval costs ([Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/industry/news/raymond-realty-enters-south-mumbai-with-rs-8500-crore-parel-project-126071501314_1.html)).

**The one formula that anchors every JDA negotiation.** The break-even revenue share is the share at which the landowner's PV equals what you'd otherwise pay outright:

> **Break-even share = PV(outright land cost) ÷ PV(project collections)**

On REF-W, that's 17.12 ÷ 139.2 ≈ **12.3%**. Any share above that makes the JDA more expensive than buying the land, in PV terms. It can still be worth doing if capital is scarce or the land isn't for sale. That's a strategy decision, not a cost saving.

**Redevelopment: what the society gets.** In society redevelopment, the "land" belongs to the society (as owner or lessee), and members' flats are the existing "tenure". The developer's cost for the land is what members receive:
- **Additional carpet** (% over existing: 30–60% is common, depending on the market: assumption)
- **Corpus fund** (cash per member)
- **Rent** for the transit period (typically 30–48 months) and **shifting charges**
- **Hardship/brokerage** allowances, parking, and amenities
- A **bank guarantee** as security for completion

The developer's sale component is total buildable carpet minus the members' new carpet.

**Pune vs Mumbai regulations.**
- **Pune (UDCPR):** society redevelopment uses normal FSI (basic + premium + TDR + ancillary), plus UDCPR provisions that give incentives for redeveloping old/dangerous buildings (**verify current clause and incentive**).
- **Mumbai (DCPR 2034):** scheme-specific routes: 33(7)(B) for societies older than 30 years (incentive BUA without premium, the higher of 15% of existing BUA or 10 sq m per tenement: **verify**); 33(9) for clusters; 33(10) for slums; 33(5) for MHADA colonies; 33(7) for cessed buildings; 33(12)(B) for specified old buildings (**verify**).

**Consent and process.** Housing society redevelopment in Maharashtra follows the government's directives under **Section 79A** of the Maharashtra Co-operative Societies Act: GB resolutions, appointment of a PMC, tendering, and developer selection by the general body. The quorum and majority thresholds are **verify current**. SRA schemes need a minimum consent share of eligible slum dwellers (historically 51%, raised in some cases: **verify**).

## 15 interview questions with model answers

### Q1 [jda] Why would a developer choose a JDA over an outright purchase?
Three reasons. First, capital: a JDA replaces a large upfront land payment with a share of future revenue, so the same equity can start more projects. That lifts return on capital even if the margin per project falls. Second, access: many landowners won't sell outright, especially in core locations or where the land has been in the family for generations. A JDA is the only way in. Third, risk sharing: if prices fall, the landowner's share falls too. The cost is margin. On REF-W, a 22.5% revenue share would cost the developer about ₹31 cr in PV versus ₹17 cr outright. So a JDA is chosen for capital efficiency and access, not because it's cheaper. The break-even share tells you exactly how much you're paying for those benefits.

### Q2 [jda] Area share or revenue share: which does the developer prefer and why?
Most developers, especially listed ones, prefer revenue share. With area share, the landowner gets physical flats and sells them independently. That creates two sellers in one project, and the landowner often undercuts on price to sell faster. There are fights over which flats (floor, view, facing), and RERA and escrow mechanics get complicated. Revenue share keeps one seller and one price list. The developer controls launch pricing and inventory release, and the landowner benefits from price increases without disrupting sales. Area share still happens with smaller developers and landowners who want flats for family or distrust the developer's accounting. The fix there is transparency: an escrow account where the landowner's share is paid out automatically on each collection.

### Q3 [jda] What's gross vs net revenue share, and why does it matter?
Gross revenue share applies the % to total consideration received from buyers. Net applies it after deductions agreed in the JDA, typically GST (which is a pass-through tax, not revenue), brokerage, and sometimes marketing costs. The difference is material. If brokerage is 2–3% and marketing 2–3%, a "25% of net" deal is about 1.2–1.5 percentage points cheaper for the developer than "25% of gross". In negotiation, landowners focus on the headline % and developers on the base it applies to. Also define whether the share applies to parking, floor-rise and preferential location charges, and to other charges like club membership and maintenance deposits. I'd model the landowner's rupee outcome under each definition before agreeing a %.

### Q4 [jda] How do you compute the break-even revenue share for a JDA?
Compare the landowner's PV under the JDA with what you'd pay outright. The break-even share is PV of outright land cost (including stamp duty) divided by PV of project collections, both discounted at the developer's cost of capital. On REF-W: outright land plus stamp is ₹17.12 cr at time zero. Project collections of ₹194 cr, spread over 15 quarters and discounted at 14% a year, have a PV of about ₹139 cr. So the break-even share is about 12.3%. A landowner asking 20% is asking for about ₹10.7 cr more in PV than an outright sale. Whether that's worth paying depends on what the capital saved earns elsewhere. At 12.3% the developer is indifferent on cost and simply gains capital efficiency.

### Q5 [jda] A landowner wants a 40% area share in Wagholi. What's your response?
Start with numbers. On REF-W, 40% of carpet is about 1.03 lakh sq ft, worth about ₹77.6 cr at ₹7,500. The land's outright value is about ₹16 cr, and the project's total profit before any land cost is about ₹48 cr (₹31 cr profit + ₹17.1 cr land and stamp). So 40% would leave the developer with a loss. For Wagholi land values, the break-even is roughly 12% of revenue. Area share is usually quoted higher than revenue share because the landowner's flats sell later and at a discount, but 40% is still far out of range. I'd explain this respectfully with the numbers, counter at around 13–15% of revenue, and offer an upfront refundable deposit to show commitment. If they won't move, walk away.

### Q6 [jda] What is a refundable security deposit in a JDA and why is it paid?
Landowners usually ask for money upfront as proof of seriousness and to fund their own needs, since a JDA pays them only from future sales. The developer pays a security deposit, often a fixed amount per acre, refundable on completion or adjusted against the landowner's share of revenue. For the developer, it works like a partial land payment without buying the land. It's at risk if the project fails, so it should be tied to title clearance and approval milestones and secured, for example by a charge on the landowner's share. In feasibility terms, include it as an early outflow and recover it through the share adjustment. It affects IRR, even if it nets to zero over the project life.

### Q7 [jda] What clauses matter most in a JDA from the developer's side?
Title representations and indemnities, with the landowner obligated to cure defects. Conditions precedent: title, approvals and vacant possession, with long-stop dates. A clear definition of the share: gross or net, which charges it covers, and when it's paid. Pricing control by the developer, with minimum-price protection for the landowner if needed. The escrow mechanism for the landowner's share. Cost responsibilities: who pays premiums, TDR, rehab and tenants' claims. Power of attorney to the developer for approvals and sales, registered and irrevocable while the agreement stands. Termination rights and what happens to money spent. A step-in right for lenders. Dispute resolution by arbitration. And a mechanism for design changes, plus tax clauses on who bears GST and stamp duty on development rights.

### Q8 [jda] What does a society actually care about in a redevelopment offer?
Less than people think about the headline %, and more about certainty. Members fear three things: the project stalls and they're homeless paying rent; rent stops mid-way; and their new flats are worse than promised. So a strong offer combines a competitive additional carpet % with a credible delivery record, a bank guarantee sized to their risk, rent paid quarterly in advance with escalation, a realistic timeline with penalties for delay, and a clear specification. Corpus matters to older members on fixed incomes, who worry about higher maintenance. The developer who explains the process clearly in the general body meeting often beats the one with a 5% higher offer. Consistency and paperwork win societies.

### Q9 [jda] How do you build a redevelopment offer for a Pune society?
Work backwards from the target margin. First, compute total buildable carpet: plot × (basic + premium + TDR allowed by road width) × 1.6 for ancillary × carpet efficiency, plus any UDCPR redevelopment incentive (**verify**). Second, estimate total costs: construction on the full construction area, premium, TDR, ancillary, approvals, rent, corpus, shifting, marketing, finance. Third, set the free-sale revenue: sale carpet × the micro-market rate. The members' new carpet is total carpet minus sale carpet, so the maximum offer % is where margin equals the target. On my example of 12 flats of 650 sq ft on 1,500 sq m, the maximum offer at an 18% margin is about 68%. I'd offer below that (say 45–50%) to leave room for negotiation.

### Q10 [jda] Explain 33(7)(B) incentive FSI with an example.
Under DCPR 2034, a residential society older than 30 years redeveloping on the same plot gets incentive built-up area without premium: 15% of existing BUA or 10 sq m per tenement, whichever is higher (**verify current text**). Example: a society with 60 flats and 3,000 sq m of existing BUA gets the higher of 450 sq m and 600 sq m, so 600 sq m. The members' existing area plus incentive is re-accommodated. If that's still below the permissible FSI for the plot, the remainder can be added using premium FSI or TDR. Most of the free-sale component comes from that remainder, and from fungible compensatory area on premium. So the incentive helps, but the economics depend on the gap between existing BUA and permissible FSI.

### Q11 [jda] What's cluster redevelopment (33(9)) and why is it attractive?
Cluster redevelopment, or urban renewal, lets a large contiguous area with many old buildings be redeveloped together, with higher FSI than individual plots and planning benefits: wider roads, open spaces and better layouts. The threshold area and consent requirements are set in the regulation (**verify current**). For a developer, clusters offer scale and design efficiency. For the city, better infrastructure. The difficulty is assembly: multiple societies and owners must agree, and one holdout can block it. Rustomjee's FY26 additions included a Lokhandwala cluster ([TipRanks](https://www.tipranks.com/news/company-announcements/keystone-realtors-posts-record-pre-sales-doubles-redevelopment-pipeline-in-fy26)). Whether it was under 33(9) or several 33(7)(B) proposals combined, I'd check in their disclosures.

### Q12 [jda] What's different about SRA (33(10)) economics?
In a slum rehabilitation scheme, the developer builds free tenements (typically around 300 sq ft carpet: **verify current**) for eligible slum dwellers, and in return gets a sale component: FSI for free-sale flats, with the ratio linked to the land's ready-reckoner rate. The rehab and sale buildings can be on the same or separate plots. There's no land payment, but the costs are rehab construction, transit accommodation or rent for dwellers, infrastructure, and long timelines. Risks: eligibility disputes (Annexure II), consent (a minimum share of eligible dwellers must agree), changes in policy, and political sensitivity. Sale can't run far ahead of rehab. Approvals are often tied to handing over rehab tenements first. Margins can be high, but the IRR is usually hurt by time.

### Q13 [jda] What's a development management (DM) deal and when does it make sense?
In a DM deal, the landowner keeps ownership, funds the project, and takes market risk. The developer provides its brand, design, approvals, construction management and sales for a fee, typically a % of revenue. The Godrej Properties–Godrej & Boyce arrangement at Vikhroli pays a fee of 10% of total revenue ([Moneylife](https://moneylife.in/article/godrej-properties-signs-agreement-with-godrej-and-boyce/20351.html)). It makes sense when the landowner has land and capital but no development capability, and wants most of the upside: typically a corporate with surplus land, or a family office. For the developer it's capital-light and fee-based, with a return on capital that's close to infinite. But the developer's brand is at risk if the landowner underfunds. So DM contracts need funding commitments and escrows.

### Q14 [jda] What's a PAAA and why must it be registered?
A Permanent Alternate Accommodation Agreement is the agreement between the developer and each existing member in a redevelopment. It specifies the new flat (carpet area, floor, specification), the transit rent, the corpus, the timeline and remedies. Registration gives it legal force and stamp duty compliance, and protects both sides: members get an enforceable right to the new flat, and the developer gets a clear record that members consented to vacate on these terms. Stamp duty on PAAAs has specific concessional treatment in Maharashtra for the rehab component (**verify current**). Operationally, having every member's PAAA signed and registered before demolition avoids the classic problem of a member who changes terms after the building is vacated.

### Q15 [jda] How would you protect a developer if some members refuse to vacate?
Prevention first. Individual consents early, a clear general body resolution meeting the required threshold under the Section 79A directives (**verify current**), and addressing specific members' issues (elderly members, medical needs, a shop owner's business loss) one to one before it becomes a dispute. Contractually, the development agreement should put the obligation to deliver vacant possession on the society, with extensions of the timeline for delays caused by dissenting members. Legally, co-operative law and courts have upheld majority decisions in several cases, with remedies for eviction of dissenting members when the majority has approved redevelopment. But these take time, so they're a last resort. Commercially, don't start rent or demolition until all flats are vacated.

## 5 numeric problems

### P1 [jda] Break-even revenue share on REF-W
**Given:** REF-W. Outright land ₹16 cr + 7% stamp = ₹17.12 cr at quarter 0. Collections ₹193.9 cr over 15 quarters (10% on booking + 90% linked to construction). Discount rate 14% per year (3.33% per quarter).

**Solution:**
- PV of collections = **₹139.2 cr** (from the REF-W cash flow script)
- Break-even share = 17.12 ÷ 139.2 = **12.3%**
- Check: a 22.5% share (Lodha Wakad's headline) pays the landowner 22.5% × 193.9 = ₹43.6 cr nominal, **PV ₹31.3 cr**. That's ₹14.2 cr more than outright.
- Developer's unlevered IRR under a 22.5% revenue-share JDA on REF-W: **9.5%**, with profit ₹9.5 cr (vs 23.6% and ₹36 cr pre-finance outright).

**Point to make:** Wakad supports 22.5% because Wakad land and prices are higher relative to construction cost. For Wagholi-level land values, the landowner's fair share is much lower. The share must be derived from the micro-market's land value, not copied from headlines.

### P2 [jda] Area share vs revenue share equivalence
**Given (assumptions):** the landowner is offered 20% area share or X% revenue share. Under area share, the landowner sells their flats 2 quarters later than the developer and at a 5% discount (reflecting their weaker brand and a smaller sales team). Discount rate 14% per year.

**Solution:**
- Value of area share relative to developer-sold revenue = 20% × 0.95 × (1/(1.0333)^2) = 20% × 0.95 × 0.9366 = **17.8%**
- So 20% area share ≈ **17.8% revenue share** in PV terms for the landowner.
- For the developer, area share is worse than the pure 20% arithmetic suggests. The landowner's discounting can drag the developer's own price, which isn't captured here.

**Point to make:** when a landowner compares "20% area" with "18% revenue", they're nearly equivalent in value, and revenue share removes the pricing conflict. That's the pitch.

### P3 [jda] Pune society: maximum offer at target margin
**Given (assumptions):** 1,500 sq m plot on a 15–24 m road (TDR 0.9). UDCPR FSI 1.1 + 0.5 + 0.9 = 2.5, plus 60% ancillary (no special redevelopment incentive assumed: **verify** whether one applies). 12 members × 650 sq ft carpet = 7,800 sq ft existing. Sale rate ₹14,000/sq ft carpet (a core Pune assumption). Construction ₹3,000/sq ft on construction area = 1.3 × FSI area. TDR ₹2,500/sq ft. ASR land ₹60,000/sq m. Premium at 50% of ASR, ancillary at 10% of ASR. Rent ₹35,000/month × 36 months. Corpus ₹10 lakh per member. Statutory ₹150/sq ft of FSI area. Professional fees 4% of construction. Marketing 5% and admin 2% of sale revenue. Finance ₹2 cr.

**Solution at a 40% offer:**
- FSI area = 1,500 × 2.5 × 1.6 = 6,000 sq m = 64,583 sq ft → carpet (78%) = 50,375 sq ft
- Rehab = 7,800 × 1.40 = 10,920 sq ft → sale carpet = 39,455 sq ft → revenue = **₹55.24 cr**
- Costs (₹ cr): construction 25.19, TDR 3.63, premium 2.25, ancillary 1.35, rent 1.51, corpus 1.20, statutory 0.97, professional fees 1.01, marketing 2.76, admin 1.10, finance 2.00 → **total 42.98**
- Profit = **₹12.26 cr = 22.2%** of sale revenue

**Sensitivity of margin to the offer:** 40% → 22.2%; 60% → 19.3%; 80% → 16.1%; 100% → 12.7%. **Maximum offer at an 18% margin ≈ 68%.**

**Point to make:** a large plot relative to existing carpet (a low-density old society) is what makes redevelopment work. Run the same numbers with 24 members of 700 sq ft on a 1,200 sq m plot on a 12 m road and the project loses about ₹15 cr at a 40% offer without an incentive (sale carpet only 12,750 sq ft against costs of about ₹33 cr). Plot-to-existing-carpet is the first ratio to check.

### P4 [jda] 33(7)(B) incentive BUA
**Given:** a Mumbai society, 60 tenements, existing BUA 3,000 sq m, older than 30 years.

**Solution:**
- 15% × 3,000 = 450 sq m. 10 sq m × 60 = 600 sq m. Incentive = **600 sq m** (the higher).
- Members' entitlement pool (existing + incentive) = 3,600 sq m of BUA, without premium.
- If permissible FSI on the plot is (say) 6,500 sq m, then 2,900 sq m can be added through premium FSI/TDR, plus fungible area on premium (**verify DCPR rates**).

**Point to make:** members often expect the incentive to be "their" extra area. How much of it is passed on as additional carpet is a negotiation, not a rule.

### P5 [jda] Security deposit effect on JDA IRR
**Given (assumptions):** a JDA on REF-W at a 13% revenue share (near break-even). The landowner demands a ₹5 cr refundable deposit at quarter 0, adjusted evenly against their share over collection quarters 3–14.

**Solution (method):**
- The deposit is an outflow of ₹5 cr at Q0 and inflows of about ₹0.42 cr per quarter over 12 quarters.
- PV of the adjustments at 3.33% per quarter, starting Q3 = 0.4167 × annuity factor(12 quarters, 3.33%) ÷ 1.0333² ≈ 0.4167 × 9.76 ÷ 1.0677 ≈ ₹3.81 cr.
- Cost to the developer in PV ≈ 5 − 3.81 = **₹1.19 cr**, about 0.9% of PV collections (₹139.2 cr). That's the same as raising the revenue share by about 0.9 percentage points.

**Point to make:** a deposit isn't free money for the landowner or a free concession for the developer. Price it as an equivalent share and trade one against the other.

## Common traps and wrong answers
- **"JDA is cheaper than buying land."** Usually it isn't, in PV terms. It's more capital-efficient.
- **Copying headline shares across markets.** 22.5% at Wakad doesn't mean 22.5% at Wagholi.
- **Ignoring gross vs net** revenue share definitions.
- **Presenting a redevelopment offer as just a %.** Rent, corpus, timeline and the bank guarantee drive the decision as much.
- **Assuming 33(7)(B) incentive is a gift to members.** It funds the project. Its allocation is negotiated.
- **Forgetting the rehab construction cost.** Members' flats cost the same to build as sale flats.
- **Starting rent before vacant possession of all flats.**
- **Giving tax or stamp duty rates on JDAs with confidence.** Defer to "I'd confirm with tax" (GST on development rights and stamp duty on JDAs are specialist areas and change: **verify current**).
